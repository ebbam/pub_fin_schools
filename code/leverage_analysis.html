REFINED MECHANISM DESCRIPTIONS BASED ON EVIDENCE:

AMPLIFICATION MECHANISM (6 states: SD, IN, LA, FL, OK, CO)

Confirmed characteristics: 1. Higher local revenue reliance (40% vs 36% Insulation, 29% Equalization) 2. Procyclical spending (boom-bust gap = 0.013, largest among mechanisms) 3. State transfers unresponsive to wage changes (elasticity ≈ +0.12)

Interpretation: In Amplification states, local wage growth translates directly into spending increases because state transfer formulas do not adjust to offset changes in local fiscal capacity. When local economies boom, education spending rises; when they bust, spending falls. This creates a ‘rich-get-richer’ dynamic where affluent commuting zones can invest more in education during periods of wage growth, while economically stagnant regions fall further behind.

Policy implication: Reinforces spatial inequality in educational resources.

INSULATION MECHANISM (3 states: KY, PA, ND)

Confirmed characteristics: 1. Medium local revenue reliance (36%) 2. Most stable/countercyclical spending (boom-bust gap = -0.004, only negative) 3. Stable state transfers (elasticity ≈ +0.22, but spending still buffered)

Interpretation: In Insulation states, education spending is effectively decoupled from local economic volatility through stable state funding formulas. Even though state transfers don’t actively offset wage changes (slight positive elasticity), the strong state funding role creates spending stability. Districts are protected from local boom-bust cycles, maintaining relatively consistent educational resources regardless of local wage fluctuations.

Policy implication: Promotes stability and prevents inequality from worsening, though local economic success doesn’t translate into improved resources.

EQUALIZATION MECHANISM (1 state: OR)

Confirmed characteristics: 1. Lowest local revenue reliance (29%) 2. Strong countercyclical state transfers (elasticity = -0.30, highly significant) 3. State transfers systematically decline when local wages rise

Interpretation: Oregon demonstrates an aggressive fiscal equalization system where state transfer formulas respond negatively to local capacity changes. When wages rise in a commuting zone, the state cuts transfers to redirect resources to lower-capacity areas. This creates active redistribution from prosperous to struggling regions, though at the cost of severing the link between local economic success and local educational investment.

Policy implication: Actively redistributive, representing the most aggressive approach to educational equity through fiscal policy.

NOTE: Oregon is a single-state example. Further research needed to identify whether other states operate similar equalization mechanisms.

Colorado

Colorado is a remarkable state precisely because education spending almost perfectly traces wages.

Colorado is one of ten states identified has having a positive causal wage-education spending relationship. Examining its commuting zones in detail confirms this pattern, wherein commuting zones whose wages follow a stable pattern exhibit stable spending, whereas those whose wages fluctuate considerably from a smooth trend show more volatile spending patterns. Though there is evidence that the spending relationship is asymmetric. In other words, local sources increase education spending in “good” times but are partially offset by intergovernmental spending in “bad” times. However, this is far from a consistently responsive mechanism, as can be seen in commuting zone 663 where IG expenditure increases despite significant local booms to spending. Altogether, Colorado’s pattern is consistent with an amplification mechanism, wherein local wage booms and busts contribute to spending increases and cuts, though the cuts are offset in certain commuting zones by intergovernmental expenditure responses. The concern remains that this intergovernmental response seems to be inconsistent across commuting zones. For example, in CZ 663 (Jackson County, CO (1 school - 1 district) & Carbon County, WY (11 schools - 2 districts)), IG expenditure increases despite significant local booms to spending, whereas in CZ 633 (Gunnison (1 district - 7 schools) and Hinsdale (1 school - 1 district) Counties), IG expenditure steps in to correct for local resource losses post-2012; and CZ 693 (Baca County - 11 schools across 5 public school districts) sees both IG spending move in tandem with local resource changes, meaning that the intergovernmental response is not reliable during local fiscal downturns.

Florida

The spending jump Franklin County, Florida, likely comes from a significant capital expenditure allocated in the 2007-2008 budget: https://www.leg.state.fl.us/data/committees/Senate/EA/EdFundingsummary_2007.pdf.

Florida represents a state in which spending co-moves with local economic conditions (GDP and wages). Essentially, spending increases prior to 2008, decreased after, and has slowly been recovering since. However, certain commuting zones still spend more per student than others, largely as a factor of uneven local revenues feeding into school systems in boom periods without equalizing intergovernmental revenues. Notably, intergovernmental revenue has become more correcting post-2008. Debate within Florida has been waged regarding the burden of public education funding (taxation at the local or state level), with state legislators preferring to push the burden to local communities, aligning with the data visualized in the below figure, where spending growth lags due to slowly recovering local economic conditions post-2008 and intergovernmental revenue decreasing across nearly all commuting zones. Essentially, in Florida (mainly post-2008), intergovernmental revenues have not served an equalizing purpose, but has rather dwindled in favor of a reliance on local funding mechanisms, leaving budgets susceptible to fluctuations in local conditions and the economic geography within the state.

South Dakota

Dips in total commuting-zone level wages in South Dakota are linked to wage losses in the construction industry (CZ 556 and 680); whereas other regions seeing similar construction-wage based dips are outweighed by the stability of wages in other sectors. Interesting cases are Pierre, SD (CZ6); Watertown, SD, whose spending increases significantly post-2008 (CZ301); Aberdeen, SD (324); Brookings, SD (467); CZ646 Boom in 2016/17; CZ 260 - major local resource losses offset by intergovernmental revenues.

There are four commuting zones that receive above-mean levels of intergovernmental transfers (260 (Dewey and Ziebach Counties), 556 (Bennett and Shannon Counties), 459 (Mellette & Todd Counties), 474 (Sioux, Cambell, Corson, Walworth Counties)). These are school districts that lie within Indian/Native American Reservations within the rural corridor of the state.

The figure demonstrating the fiscal decomposition of spending changes displays commuting zones, ordered from highest to lowest spending per pupil where the black outline demonstrates the zones in the top 25th percentile of wage growth variance. Of the top five commuting zones in terms of wage variance, three include those counties within Reservations. The remaining two rely largely on wages from Construction and Agricultural industries.

Indiana

Indiana’s public education system underwent two major shifts to its funding landscape during the time period analyzed in this study. First, the Indiana General Assembly passed Public Law 148 n 2008 which eliminated property taxes as a revenue source for the state General Fund, the main vehicle funding school operating expenses. This legal change is demonstrated clearly in the figures below, wherein the intergovernmental transfers across all commuting zones exhibits a distinct mean shift [Michael 2009; Egalite & Catt 2025]. . Second, Indiana has championed “school choice” reform programs wherein the state has, among other reforms, subsidized access to private education through publicly funded voucher programs and established several charter schools which aim to provide independently operated public schools. Critics of “school choice” reform programs cite the potential for charter schools or voucher programs to crowd out traditional public school funding (Wang et al; Shaffer; Bifulco), while proponents cite the ability to adapt to heterogeneous student needs, the benefit of a more direct student funding channel (families and students themselves decide where to spend their allotted funding dollars); and scant empirical evidence of harms to students remaining in traditional public schools (Bitler; Egalite; Wang; Egalite & Catt).

However, evident in the below figures, public school funding has decreased significantly across nearly all commuting zones within Indiana following 2008. Though intergovernmental revenues have largely balanced the losses in local funding sources, public education expenditure remains at a lower level in both real and nominal terms toward the end of the time series analyzed in this study.

All Other States

For the states where no statistically significant causal effect is detected, we proceed with an analysis of the potential source of the negligible effect. This diagnosis is important for two reasons. First, a near-zero elasticity can indicate the presence of strong intergovernmental equalization tools to alleviate the spill-over consequences of uneven wage distributions within a state. Second, a near-zero elasticity can indicate a fundamental decoupling between commuting-zone level economic conditions. Both of these sources of a negligible elasticity would provide potential support for the wider implementation of the relevant state or local public education funding mechanisms as they effectively insulate commuting zones from a wage-based channel for public education inequity. However, a near-zero elasticity could additionally indicate either a poorly estimated functional form unfit for the research question or insufficient within-state variation to detect meaningful differences between higher- versus lower-wage commuting zones. In either of these latter cases, it will be important to highlight any statistical-, data-, or estimation-related obstacles to identification to ensure that undue conclusions about the quality of local public education funding systems are not drawn from this effect. In other words, distinguishing between states in which a non-zero elasticity has meaningful policy relevance or is simply an artifact of a poor econometric specification or insufficient data is imperative to ensure the integrity of conclusions drawn.
